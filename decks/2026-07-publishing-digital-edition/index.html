01

What does it mean to publish a digital edition?

~1 min. Collect two or three answers from the room before advancing — the chips on the next slides will echo them. Angle: “public” is the word doing the work in “publication”.

The XML is not the edition

An edition sitting in a repo is not published.

published = viewable findable citable durable

~2–3 min. The provocation, right after their answers: encoding is not publishing.

  • Sahle’s test — a digitized edition is not a digital edition: if you can print it without loss of information or functionality, it was never really digital. The edition = text + encoding + presentation + access working together — not the file.
  • Counterweight — Turska/Cummings/Rahtz, jTEI 2016 (“myth of presentation”): bespoke interfaces rot; the source is the scholarship.
  • Synthesis — the source is the scholarship, but publication is a separate, deliberate act layered on top.
  • Chips — reveal one by one; tie each back to something a participant said.

Published means…

  • viewable — a human can read it, not just a parser
  • findable — metadata and a place where people discover it
  • citable — a stable, versioned reference to point at
  • durable — it outlives the server, the grant, the laptop

~2 min. The four adjectives are the map of the session.

  • Mapping — display → viewable; metadata + DOI → findable, citable; archiving → durable.
  • Why four extra words — print bundled all of this invisibly; the web unbundles it, so each property must be built deliberately.

~2 min. The formal version of the four adjectives.

  • Birthplace — Lorentz Center, Leiden, 27–31 Jan 2014: workshop “Jointly Designing a Data FAIRport” (Barend Mons), ~30 people; life scientists tired of data sharing meaning “email the corresponding author and pray”.
  • Paper — Wilkinson et al., Scientific Data, March 2016 — among the most-cited papers of the decade.
  • Political uptake — within months: G20 communiqué 2016, EU Horizon programmes — why every EU grant application asks about FAIR.
  • FAIR ≠ open — “as open as possible, as closed as necessary”: data may be restricted, the metadata must be open. A rights-encumbered edition can still be FAIR.
  • Mapping — I is done (a week of TEI); today supplies F, A and R: DOI, hosting, licence, versioned release.

  • Preservation — hosting is not archiving: Zenodo + GitHub

02

From TEI to something human-readable

Transition: you have TEI — who can actually read it? One deliberately compact section: the spectrum, then on to rights.

The spectrum

browse editions:
Catalog of Digital Scholarly Editions · Catalogue of Digital Editions

The TEI file is the website: a small JavaScript library teaches the browser to display TEI tags directly, styled with plain CSS. One source, no transformation pipeline — and anything beyond display is yours to build.

Frankenstein Variorum · CETEIcean live examples

Transform once, serve forever: a generator bakes the TEI into plain HTML pages that any host can serve — GitHub Pages, for free. No moving parts after the build: nothing very little to patch, and nothing very little left to break.

DIY-frankensTEIn · Endings-compliant editions (UVic) · minimal computing

Edition-shaped software: ready-made reading environments — facsimile beside transcription, edition levels, critical apparatus, witness comparison. You configure instead of program, and the result is still just files on a web server.

Codice Pelavicino (EVT) · Versioning Machine samples · LERA demos

The edition as an application: an XML database answers readers’ queries live: full-text search, filtering, views on demand, etc. The most powerful end of the axis. Somebody must run, patch and migrate the server.

edition humboldt digital · TEI Publisher registry · TEI Publisher demos

~6–8 min with the per-family fragments. Tool names and examples are clickable.

  • Reframe the axis — it is a maintenance-promise axis: client-side promises nothing but a browser; a server platform promises a person, forever. The sin is picking heavy for a light project by default.
  • Minimal computing — GO::DH, ~2014–15, Alex Gil et al.: what can you build with no server, no budget, no sysadmin, unreliable electricity? Born from work with colleagues in Cuba / the Global South. Wax comes straight from that movement.
  • Endings Project — UVic, ~2016: the biggest threat to editions is not disk failure but dependency (the PHP upgrade, the departed developer). Hence: static output, completable projects, citable versions.
  • CETEIcean — say “cetacean”: TEI rendered in the sea of the browser (Cayless & Viglianti); TEI elements become HTML custom elements — no transformation at all.
  • EVT — born in Pisa to publish the Vercelli Book (one of the four great Old English codices). Versioning Machine — Susan Schreibman, ~2000: the veteran. LERA — Halle, semi-automatic comparison of long prose versions.
  • TEI Publisher / eXist-db — stewarded by the e-editiones society (2020); flagship: edition-humboldt.de.
  • Anchor — Edition Light (this morning) sits on the static-sites card.

03

Whose edition is it?

Transition: the edition is readable — but whose is it? Promise the shape: what flows in (text, images, process) and what flows out (your rights, your license).

Rights in, rights out

  • the text — copyright runs until 70 years after the author’s death. Your medieval scribe is safe; modern and born-digital authors usually are not.
  • the images — in the EU, a faithful scan of a public-domain work is itself public domain (DSM art. 14). But your contract with the holding institution can still restrict use.
  • the process — keystroke logs of a living writer are personal data under EU law: you need consent, not just copyright.
  • your copyright is thin — a faithful transcription adds no original creation, so it earns little or no copyright of its own.
  • …but some countries step in — Germany gives the editor a 25-year exclusive right in the edited text (§ 70 UrhG): others may not simply republish it.
  • declare it — a LICENSE file in the repo, <licence> in the teiHeader. Undeclared = all rights reserved.

~3 min. Title builds: “Rights in” + three source questions; then “, rights out” in green with the second column.

  • The text — life + 70 (EU): Conan Doyle (d. 1930) is safe; a 1990s notebook is not. Permissions-land is normal — it is why editions cluster pre-1900.
  • The images: a saga — US: Bridgeman v. Corel (1999): faithful repro = no originality (cf. Feist 1991 killing “sweat of the brow”). Germany: Reiss Engelhorn Museum sued Wikimedia (2015) over 17 photos of public-domain art; BGH sided with the museum (2018); the EU then overruled it continent-wide with DSM art. 14 (2019). The museum won the battle and lost the war.
  • But — contracts still bite: an archive can make you sign terms; contract law does not care about art. 14.
  • Thin copyright — fidelity is the opposite of originality: the better the transcription, the less protected.
  • § 70 / § 71 UrhG — scholarly editions get 25 years; and the editio princeps right (§ 71): first publication of a never-printed work = 25 years exclusive. A first edition of an unpublished medieval text is not as free as students assume.
  • The process — GDPR: keystroke logs of a living writer are personal data (pauses, deletions, 2 a.m. revisions reveal the person) → consent, not just copyright. Ties to Tuesday.

Pick a license

CC license chooser
choosealicense.com

the edition — text, encoding, images

  • CC BY reuse with attribution — the scholarly default
  • CC BY-SA …and derivatives must share alike
  • CC0 no conditions at all — the norm for metadata
  • NC ND avoid: they block teaching, anthologies, derivatives

the code — XSLT, scripts

  • MIT do anything, keep the notice — short and permissive
  • GPL-3.0 derivatives must stay open (copyleft)
  • CC not for code — Creative Commons says so itself

No license = all rights reserved — reuse is legally blocked, and the R of FAIR fails.

~3 min. Two layers, two license families.

  • CC founding myth — 1998 Sonny Bono Act (“Mickey Mouse Protection Act”) extends copyright 20 years; Lessig challenges and loses Eldred v. Ashcroft (2003); Creative Commons (founded 2001, first licenses Dec 2002) is the constructive answer: “some rights reserved”, readable by humans, lawyers and machines. Billions of works today.
  • CC BY — maps onto scholarly norms (attribute); edition humboldt digital uses BY-SA.
  • CC0 (2009) — attribution is friction at scale; nobody can attribute fifty million metadata records — the Europeana norm.
  • NC/ND — “non-commercial” is undefined (a paid summer school? a press anthology?) so cautious reusers walk away; ND forbids what scholarship is.
  • Code — CC itself says: not for software (no patent grant) → MIT (short, permissive) or GPL (copyleft).
  • Punchline — no license = all rights reserved, automatically (Berne). Public on GitHub grants exactly nothing; the R of FAIR fails by default.

04

Citable & durable: Zenodo + GitHub

Transition: readable and licensed — now make it citable and durable. This is the section the hands-on cashes out.

Hosting ≠ preservation

GitHub Pages is a website. A website is not an archive.

  • links rot — roughly one in five scholarly articles suffers reference rot; half the web links in U.S. Supreme Court opinions are dead (hence Perma.cc)
  • platforms die — GeoCities, Google Code, Bitbucket’s Mercurial repos: all gone. GitHub is a company, not a memory institution
  • preservation is a promise — an institution’s commitment, redundant storage, and a persistent identifier. Not a feature you toggle

~2 min. The money slide — every number is documented.

  • Link rot — Klein et al. 2014 (PLOS ONE): ~1 in 5 STM articles suffers reference rot. Zittrain/Albert/Lessig 2014: 50% of links in Supreme Court opinions dead, >70% in the Harvard Law Review → Harvard built Perma.cc. If even the Supreme Court’s citations rot…
  • GeoCities — founded 1994, bought by Yahoo for ~$3.6B (1999), deleted 26/27 Oct 2009: tens of millions of homepages, a whole vernacular web culture. Volunteer “Archive Team” rescue ≈ 1 TB torrent (cf. the artwork The Deleted City).
  • More corpses — Google Code (closed 2016); Bitbucket deleted every Mercurial repo (2020).
  • One-liner — GitHub is a company, not a memory institution: companies have exits, institutions have mandates.
  • Ask — what survives your GitHub account, your funding, your career?

The archive layer

Zenodo

the deposit

  • CERN-run — stored in the CERN Data Centre; retained for the lifetime of the lab
  • free — up to 50 GB per record; the DOI registers within seconds
  • GitHub-aware — one switch: every release archived, each with its own version DOI

belts and braces

free extras

  • Software Heritage — already archives every public GitHub repo, automatically
  • Wayback Machine — snapshots the rendered site; Zenodo keeps files, not web pages
  • your library — institutional repositories add an institutional commitment

~2 min. The deposit and the free extras.

  • Zenodo — launched 8 May 2013 as successor of the OpenAIRE “Orphan Records Repository” (research with no institutional home); built and run by CERN on Invenio(RDM).
  • CERN Data Centre — deposits live next to LHC data, some of the most redundantly kept bits on Earth; retention “for the lifetime of the laboratory” (programme defined for 20+ years); 50 GB per record, free.
  • Software Heritage — Inria (Di Cosmo & Zacchiroli), announced 30 June 2016, UNESCO partnership 2017; mission: archive all public source code — 20+ billion source files. Their repos are probably already in it.
  • Wayback Machine — Brewster Kahle, crawling since 1996: keeps the rendered site; Zenodo keeps files, not web pages. Complementary layers.

The GitHub → Zenodo flow

~3 min. The full loop, no command line: authorize once, flip the toggle, make a release (tag = version number); Zenodo picks it up in minutes. Red deposit ≈ JSON syntax error.

  • DOI = name, not address — built on the Handle System (Bob Kahn, co-inventor of TCP/IP); DOI Foundation ~2000; Zenodo mints via DataCite (2009). Links rot because they couple identity to location; a DOI decouples them — a promise with an institution behind it.
  • semver — Tom Preston-Werner (GitHub co-founder), ~2010. For editions: MAJOR = text/structure changed (citations may break), MINOR = content added, PATCH = corrections.
  • Point — versions turn “it changed since I cited it” from a scandal into a citation practice.

The GitHub → Zenodo flow

The real interface: Zenodo’s own onboarding panel says it in three steps too — flip the switch, create a release, get the badge. This is the page they will see after linking GitHub; shown live in the hands-on.

Declare yourself


{
  "title": "My Digital Edition",
  "creators": [
    { "name": "Last, First",
      "orcid": "0000-0000-0000-0000" }
  ],
  "license": "cc-by-4.0"
}

.zenodo.json — what Zenodo reads at deposit time. Without it, Zenodo guesses 


cff-version: 1.2.0
message: "If you use this edition, cite it."
title: "My Digital Edition"
authors:
  - family-names: Last
    given-names: First
license: CC-BY-4.0

CITATION.cff — what GitHub reads: adds a “Cite this repository” button (citation-file-format.github.io)

the license — the choice you just made, written where machines can read it: the deposit displays it, and reuse is unambiguous

your identity — an ORCID is a persistent identifier for you: it survives name changes and moves, and ties the edition to its editor

~3 min. Metadata, or: the tag on the scroll.

  • The name Zenodo — after Zenodotus of Ephesus, first head librarian of the Library of Alexandria (3rd c. BC) and the first recorded systematic metadata: alphabetical order, tags on scrolls recording author and title. A .zenodo.json is the same act, 2,300 years on.
  • Practical — both files in the repo root, dot included; “contributors” (unlike “creators”) need a “type” field — the classic silent red-deposit failure.
  • CITATION.cff — the GitHub-native sibling: adds the “Cite this repository” button; message and cff-version are required; both files can coexist.
  • ORCID (2012) — a DOI for people: survives name changes, moves, and the world’s many scholars named Wang Wei. Two minutes, free, permanent.

Wear the badge


[![DOI](https://img.shields.io/badge/DOI-<doi>-blue)](https://doi.org/<doi>)

One line in the README: the edition announces its own citable, archived self.

shields.io renders badges for anything — DOI, license, release — as simple, reliable images

~1 min. One line in the README: the edition announces its own citable, archived self.

  • Why shields.io — a static badge: the DOI never changes, so nothing needs to be live. (Backstory if asked: GitHub proxies README images through camo; Zenodo’s badge server is rate-limited, camo caches the failure → broken icon.)
  • Detail — the slash in the DOI must be URL-encoded (%2F). Closes the FAIR loop: F and R visible on the repo homepage.

Aside: anonymous, for now

Sometimes the repo must not name you — yet.

  • double-blind review — the journal wants the edition, without the editor
  • Anonymous GitHub — an anonymized mirror of your repo: names, links and paths scrubbed, one shareable URL, with an expiration date
  • after acceptance — de-anonymize, release, deposit: the DOI takes over the identity work

~1 min. anonymous.4open.science — built for double-blind software review: an anonymized mirror, names/links/paths scrubbed, one shareable URL, expiration date.

  • Symmetry to end on — identity is something you manage: claim it forever with a DOI and ORCID, or withhold it for a while with a mirror.

05

Hands-on: archive your edition

Say it plainly: this morning you made an edition; in twenty minutes it will be citable. Sandbox accounts ready; Nooshin and Lamyk circulate.

This morning you made an edition. Now make it citable.

  1. Sign up at sandbox.zenodo.org (via GitHub)
  2. Flip the switch on your edition repo
  3. Add a .zenodo.json to the repo root
  4. Create a release (tag = version number)
  5. Admire your DOI — and grab the badge

~15–20 min, with Nooshin and Lamyk supporting. Sandbox = safe playground; real Zenodo later at home.

  • Most common failure — JSON syntax error → deposit fails → fix, draft a new release.
  • Fallback — if time is short: live demo in 5 min, students replicate afterwards.
